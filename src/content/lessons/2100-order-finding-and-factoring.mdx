Shor's algorithm (next lesson) is famous for factoring integers, but the quantum part of it doesn't touch factoring directly. It solves a completely different-looking problem, **order-finding**, and this lesson is the classical number theory that connects the two: a reduction that was already known before Shor, his contribution was making order-finding itself efficient quantumly, using exactly the QPE circuit from last lesson.

## The Order of a mod N

For integers $a$ and $N$ with $\gcd(a,N)=1$, the **order** of $a$ modulo $N$, written $r$, is the smallest positive integer such that

$$a^r \equiv 1 \pmod N$$

Such an $r$ always exists when $\gcd(a,N)=1$ (a consequence of Euler's theorem), and the powers $a^1, a^2, \ldots, a^r=1, a^{r+1}=a, \ldots$ cycle with period exactly $r$.

## From Order to Factors

Suppose $N$ is composite (not prime, not a prime power) and you've found the order $r$ of some $a$ modulo $N$. If $r$ is **even** and $a^{r/2} \not\equiv -1 \pmod N$, then both

$$\gcd\!\left(a^{r/2}-1,\,N\right) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \gcd\!\left(a^{r/2}+1,\,N\right)$$

are **nontrivial factors of $N$** (neither 1 nor $N$ itself). Why: $a^r \equiv 1 \pmod N$ means $N$ divides $a^r - 1 = (a^{r/2}-1)(a^{r/2}+1)$, but (given the two conditions above) $N$ divides neither factor on its own, so $N$'s prime factors must be split between the two, and the gcd with each side pulls exactly that split out. For a randomly chosen $a$ coprime to $N$, this works (gives a valid $r$, even, with $a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1$) with probability at least $\tfrac12$, so a few random retries make success overwhelmingly likely.

## Worked Example: N = 15, a = 7

Compute powers of 7 mod 15: $7^1=7$, $7^2=49\equiv4$, $7^3=343\equiv13$, $7^4=2401\equiv1$. So $r=4$ (even ✓). Then $a^{r/2}=7^2\equiv4 \pmod{15}$, and $4 \not\equiv -1 \equiv 14 \pmod{15}$ ✓, both conditions hold:

$$\gcd(4-1,\,15) = \gcd(3,15) = 3, \qquad \gcd(4+1,\,15) = \gcd(5,15) = 5$$

$3 \times 5 = 15$. Factored, using nothing but the order $r=4$ and two classical gcd computations, both efficient (Euclid's algorithm).

## Order-Finding as a Unitary

Finding $r$ classically (trying powers of $a$ one at a time) takes exponentially many steps in the number of digits of $N$; this is the actual hard part real-world factoring relies on. Quantum Phase Estimation can find $r$ efficiently, given the right unitary. Define $U_a$ on a register large enough to hold values $0,\ldots,N-1$:

$$U_a|x\rangle = |ax \bmod N\rangle$$

$U_a$ is unitary for the same reason `X` is: it's a *permutation* of basis states (no two different $x$ map to the same $ax \bmod N$, since $a$ is invertible mod $N$), and every permutation matrix is automatically unitary, its inverse is just running the permutation backwards, $U_a^{-1} = U_{a^{-1}\bmod N}$.

## Why Its Eigenvalues Encode r

$U_a$'s eigenvectors turn out to be

$$|u_s\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt r}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1} e^{-2\pi i sk/r}\,\big|a^k \bmod N\big\rangle, \qquad s = 0, 1, \ldots, r-1$$

with eigenvalue $e^{2\pi i s/r}$ (check: applying $U_a$ shifts every $a^k\bmod N$ to $a^{k+1}\bmod N$, i.e. relabels the sum's index, which pulls out exactly a factor of $e^{2\pi is/r}$ thanks to the phases already built into $|u_s\rangle$'s definition). Feed $U_a$ and any single $|u_s\rangle$ (for $s\neq0$) into QPE from last lesson, and it returns $\theta = s/r$: a fraction with $r$ sitting in the denominator, recoverable from $\theta$ by a classical algorithm called **continued fractions**, without ever needing to know $r$ up front. Next lesson covers the one remaining wrinkle, preparing $|u_s\rangle$ without already knowing $r$, and puts every piece from this and the last three lessons together into the complete algorithm.
