Quantum amplitudes are **complex numbers**, not just probabilities, and quantum states are **vectors** built from them. Everything in this course, gates, superposition, interference, eigenvalues, is linear algebra over the complex numbers. This lesson is the toolkit; the next four build on it one piece at a time before the course touches a single line of Qiskit.

## Complex Numbers

A complex number is $z = a + bi$, where $a$ (the *real part*) and $b$ (the *imaginary part*) are ordinary real numbers and $i$ satisfies $i^2 = -1$. There's no "solving for $i$"; it's simply defined into existence because $x^2 = -1$ has no real solution, and it turns out to be exactly the right extra ingredient quantum mechanics needs.

Two operations matter most for this course:

- **Complex conjugate**: $z^* = a - bi$, flip the sign of the imaginary part.
- **Modulus (absolute value)**: $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, and conveniently $|z|^2 = z z^*$, since $(a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 + b^2$.

That second identity is the one you'll use constantly: whenever a lesson says "the probability of measuring this outcome is $|\alpha|^2$," this is exactly what's being computed.

## Polar Form and Phase

Every complex number can also be written as $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = re^{i\theta}$ (Euler's formula), where $r = |z|$ is the modulus and $\theta$ is the **phase**, the angle $z$ makes on the complex plane. Multiplying two complex numbers in this form just adds their phases and multiplies their moduli: $r_1 e^{i\theta_1} \cdot r_2 e^{i\theta_2} = r_1 r_2 \, e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}$.

This is why quantum computing talks about "phase" so much: a gate that only changes an amplitude's phase (leaves $r$ alone) doesn't change any measurement probability at all, since $|re^{i\theta}|^2 = r^2$ regardless of $\theta$, but phase differences between amplitudes are exactly what produce interference once you combine them, which is the entire mechanism later lessons (starting with superposition and interference) are built on.

## Vectors

A qubit's state is a column vector with complex entries, living in $\mathbb{C}^2$:

$$|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}$$

$n$ qubits together live in $\mathbb{C}^{2^n}$ (the Tensor Products lesson later in this track covers exactly how that space is built up). The ket notation $|\psi\rangle$ is just a label for "this is a vector"; $|0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ and $|1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$ are the two vectors you'll see the most.

## Inner Product and Norm

The **inner product** of two vectors $|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1\\\alpha_2\end{pmatrix}$ and $|\phi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}\beta_1\\\beta_2\end{pmatrix}$ is

$$\langle\psi|\phi\rangle = \alpha_1^*\beta_1 + \alpha_2^*\beta_2$$

The bra $\langle\psi|$ is $|\psi\rangle$'s entries, conjugated and laid out as a row instead of a column; $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle$ is literally that row times $|\phi\rangle$'s column, ordinary matrix multiplication. Conjugating the first vector is what makes $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ come out real and non-negative for every vector, which is exactly what makes the next definition work:

$$\||\psi\rangle\| = \sqrt{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle} = \sqrt{|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2}$$

This is the **norm**, and it's why every valid quantum state satisfies $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$: a state vector is required to have norm 1, since $|\alpha|^2$ and $|\beta|^2$ are measurement probabilities that must sum to 1.

Two vectors are **orthogonal** when $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle = 0$. Check $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$: $\langle 0|1\rangle = 1^*\cdot 0 + 0^*\cdot 1 = 0$. They're orthogonal, and each has norm 1, together making them **orthonormal**, exactly what you want from the two basis states of a bit.
