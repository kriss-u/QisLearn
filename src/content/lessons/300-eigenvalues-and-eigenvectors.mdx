Most vectors, hit with a matrix, come out pointing in some new direction entirely. A special few don't: the matrix only stretches or flips them, without changing the direction they point in. Those special vectors, and the amount they get scaled by, turn out to matter enormously for quantum computing, all the way up to Quantum Phase Estimation and Shor's algorithm later in this course.

## The Definition

For a matrix $A$, a nonzero vector $v$ is an **eigenvector** of $A$ if

$$Av = \lambda v$$

for some scalar $\lambda$, called the corresponding **eigenvalue**. $A$ doesn't rotate $v$ into some other direction; it just multiplies it by $\lambda$.

## Finding Them: the Characteristic Equation

Rearranging $Av = \lambda v$ gives $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$. A nonzero $v$ satisfying this only exists when $A - \lambda I$ is singular (non-invertible), which for a $2\times2$ matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix}$ means its determinant is zero:

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = (a-\lambda)(d-\lambda) - bc = 0$$

This is the **characteristic equation**; its roots are $A$'s eigenvalues.

## Worked Example: the $Z$ Gate

$Z = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}$. The characteristic equation is $(1-\lambda)(-1-\lambda) = 0$, giving $\lambda = 1$ or $\lambda = -1$.

- For $\lambda = 1$: solving $(Z - I)v = 0$ gives $v = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = |0\rangle$. Check: $Z|0\rangle = |0\rangle = 1\cdot|0\rangle$. ✓
- For $\lambda = -1$: solving gives $v = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} = |1\rangle$. Check: $Z|1\rangle = -|1\rangle$ (you computed exactly this in the last lesson's quiz). ✓

$Z$'s eigenvectors are just $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, with eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$: unsurprising, since $Z$ was already built to leave the computational basis states alone (up to sign).

## Worked Example: the $X$ Gate

$X = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ has the same characteristic equation, $-(1-\lambda^2)\cdot(-1) $ simplifies to $\lambda^2 = 1$, so again $\lambda = \pm1$, but this time the eigenvectors aren't $|0\rangle$/$|1\rangle$ ($X$ visibly swaps those, it doesn't leave them alone). Solving $(X-I)v=0$ and $(X+I)v=0$ gives:

$$|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} \text{ for } \lambda=+1, \qquad |-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} \text{ for } \lambda=-1$$

That second line, $X|-\rangle = -|-\rangle$, is exactly the fact the phase-kickback lesson leans on entirely, stated there without the "eigenvalue" name. Now you have the formal machinery behind it: $|-\rangle$ is an eigenvector of $X$ with eigenvalue $-1$, full stop, and that's *why* applying $X$ to it only ever multiplies it by $-1$ instead of moving it somewhere else.

## Eigenbasis

$|0\rangle,|1\rangle$ form a complete basis of eigenvectors for $Z$ (its **eigenbasis**); $|+\rangle,|-\rangle$ form one for $X$. Every $2\times2$ matrix relevant to this course has an eigenbasis like this: a basis of eigenvectors that the matrix acts on by pure scaling, no rotation. Later, Quantum Phase Estimation (in the algorithms track) is built entirely around a single question: given an eigenvector of some unitary $U$, what is its eigenvalue? Since a unitary's eigenvalues always have the form $e^{i\theta}$ (the Unitary Matrices lesson right after this one covers why), "find the eigenvalue" really means "find the phase $\theta$," which is where that lesson's name comes from.
