Grover's algorithm was the last lesson built from tools you already had, $H$, $X$, $CZ$. The next two algorithms, Quantum Phase Estimation and Shor's algorithm, are both built around a new primitive: the **Quantum Fourier Transform** (QFT). This lesson is the transform itself; the next two put it to work.

## The Transform

The classical discrete Fourier transform turns a list of $N$ numbers into a list of $N$ frequency components. The QFT is the same idea, applied to a quantum state's amplitudes instead of a classical list, done entirely with unitary gates. It maps a computational basis state $|j\rangle$ to an equal-weighted superposition over every basis state, with each one's phase encoding $j$:

$$\text{QFT}|j\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} e^{2\pi i jk/N}|k\rangle$$

Compare this to $H$ on a single qubit ($N=2$): $H|0\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$, $H|1\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_k e^{2\pi i \cdot 1 \cdot k/2}|k\rangle$. $H$ *is* the 1-qubit QFT; the general QFT is what you get generalizing that same "spread into a phase-encoded superposition" idea to $n$ qubits, $N=2^n$.

## Requires a New Gate: Controlled-Phase

Building the $n$-qubit case needs a gate this course hasn't used yet: **controlled-phase**, $CP(\theta)$, `qc.cp(theta, control, target)`. It applies a phase rotation $P(\theta) = \text{diag}(1, e^{i\theta})$ to the target, but *only* on the branch where the control qubit is $|1\rangle$, otherwise it does nothing. $CP$ is physically symmetric in its two qubits (swapping which one you call "control" gives the identical gate), which is why circuit diagrams draw it as a plain dot on both wires rather than $CX$'s dot-and-ring.

## The 2-Qubit Circuit

For 2 qubits ($N=4$):

```python
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
import math

qc = QuantumCircuit(2)
qc.h(1)
qc.cp(math.pi / 2, 0, 1)
qc.h(0)
qc.swap(0, 1)
```

$H$ on qubit 1 starts spreading it into superposition; the controlled-phase gate lets qubit 0 imprint a phase onto that superposition depending on its own value; $H$ on qubit 0 spreads it too; the final $SWAP$ puts the two qubits back in the order the definition above expects. Try it on $|01\rangle$ ($j=1$): the amplitudes come out as $\tfrac12, \tfrac{i}2, -\tfrac12, -\tfrac{i}2$ on $|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle$ respectively, exactly $\tfrac1{\sqrt4}e^{2\pi i\cdot1\cdot k/4}$ for $k=0,1,2,3$.

## The General Pattern ($n$ Qubits)

The $n$-qubit QFT repeats the same idea per qubit: $H$, then a cascade of $CP$ gates from every later qubit, each with a smaller angle ($\pi/2$, then $\pi/4$, then $\pi/8$, ...), then move to the next qubit, finishing with a layer of $SWAP$s to reverse the qubit order. The circuit above is exactly this pattern's smallest nontrivial case, one $H$, one $CP$, one more $H$, one $SWAP$. Every extra qubit adds one more $H$ and one more $CP$ cascade, growing the gate count as $O(n^2)$, exponentially *fewer* gates than the $O(N\log N)$ the fastest classical FFT needs for the same size input, though the two aren't directly comparable: the QFT acts on amplitudes you can't read out directly, and turning that into a genuine speedup for a real problem is exactly what Quantum Phase Estimation (next lesson) does.
