Everything up to this point has used one or two qubits at a time. Quantum *algorithms* (starting with Deutsch-Jozsa a few lessons from now) get their power from doing this at scale: putting an entire register into superposition at once, then using **interference** to cancel out the amplitudes of wrong answers before you ever measure. This lesson builds both pieces.

## Hadamard on Every Qubit

You already know `qc.h([0, 1, 2])` broadcasts H across a list of qubits (from How Circuits and Gates Take Their Arguments). Do that to *every* qubit in an $n$-qubit register, starting from $|00\ldots0\rangle$, and you get a perfectly uniform superposition over all $2^n$ basis states:

$$H^{\otimes n}|0\rangle^{\otimes n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_{x=0}^{2^n-1}|x\rangle$$

Every one of the $2^n$ basis states gets exactly the same amplitude, $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}$, not approximately equal, *exactly* equal. That exactness is the whole point: it's what lets later interference cancel some of them down to exactly zero, rather than just "small."

```python
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit

qc = QuantumCircuit(3)
qc.h([0, 1, 2])
```

## Interference: When Amplitudes Cancel Exactly

Superposition alone isn't enough to make an algorithm useful: you also need a way to *steer* probability toward the right answer. That's interference: routing two different computational "paths" so their amplitudes for the same outcome add up (constructive) or cancel out (destructive).

The simplest possible example is one qubit and two Hadamards in a row. You already know $H|0\rangle = |+\rangle$. What does a *second* $H$ do?

```python
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit

qc = QuantumCircuit(1)
qc.h(0)
qc.h(0)
```

  circuit={{ numQubits: 1, gates: [{ gate: "h", qubits: [0] }, { gate: "h", qubits: [0] }] }}
  views={["circuit", "table"]}
/>

Each basis state after the second $H$ is reached by two different paths through the intermediate $|+\rangle$ state: one contributing a positive amplitude to $|1\rangle$, one contributing a negative amplitude of the same size. They cancel to exactly zero, and the state lands back on $|0\rangle$ with certainty. $H$ is its own inverse ($H^2 = I$), and this cancellation is *why*: it's not a coincidence of these particular numbers, it's interference doing exactly what it's supposed to do.

This (steering amplitude toward a "yes" answer and cancelling it away from every "no" answer) is the mechanism every algorithm in the rest of this track relies on.
